# Whole-solution audit, 31 October 2026

The product owner paused the build and asked four questions:
1. Does the solution still do what the original design intended?
2. Is it one coherent, practical product?
3. Is the plan still sensible?
4. How should Muse Wonder be used for human testing?

During the audit they added a fifth requirement, in their words: "we aren't limited to a
prefilled list of roles … I want it to feel like you have an omniscient career coach who knows
all possible jobs that could fit you". They also supplied research on ESCO's limits
(`esco_research.txt`, their own file, not committed).

**How it was done.** Six independent read-only reviews, each on the most capable model:
- the specification, capability by capability;
- the code's coherence;
- where the review effort went;
- the plan;
- the person's path through the app;
- the role space.

One synthesis, this report, owns the recommendations. Nothing in the product changed. The six
reviews are the appendices in `docs/reviews/l0-audit-2026-10-31/` (A to F; G is the Muse Wonder
notes). The product owner's decisions are on one page:
`docs/reviews/l0-audit-2026-10-31-summary.md`.

Nothing in the plan changes until the product owner approves.

---

## The short answer

1. **The foundations are sound, but the product can't be used past its first chapter.**
   - After about 58 hours of build, a person can talk, get grounded career ideas and choose
     a target.
   - Skills, Options, Routes and Plan show "not built yet".
   - So the question the product exists to answer can't be asked yet: "which achievable route
     makes the best use of my time and money?"
2. **The front door is open and the back is nearly closed.**
   - You can pick any of about 3,000 occupations.
   - But skills, options and routes exist for only the four reviewed roles: data quality analyst,
     IT support technician, registered nurse and bookkeeping assistant.
   - Nobody decided this. The live research that was meant to find options for other roles was
     never built, and no task owns it.
3. **Roles are kept to ESCO by our own rule, not by the specification.**
   - The specification says a role list is "never the universe of recommendable careers".
   - A UK list of about 24,000 job titles from ONS is already in the database. Discovery doesn't
     use it.
4. **The long review loops went to the wrong places.**
   - Rounds that fixed wrong data shown to a person were the best value, and they finished in
     one to three rounds.
   - The rounds that dragged on were about the wording of small notes and about matching rules.
   - Under 1% of all review findings were about the plain words the product owner asked for.
5. **The plan should put the decision path first.**
   - Build Skills, Options, Routes and Plan plainly, in the world as it already stands.
   - Open the role space early.
   - Add the world's special moments after the path works.
   - Total work rises only by the new role and option research. A printable plan arrives much
     sooner than in the current order.

## 2. Is it one clear, practical product?

### What earns its keep (keep it)

- **Writes:** one model-run path. Every model-backed feature is a task in it, with access, key
  and budget checked in one place. Writes are versioned and safe to retry, and ownership is
  enforced by the database itself.
- **Data:** money is a type, and "unknown" can never become zero or "none found". The
  readiness and route checks are pure code. The option pack's facts each cite their source
  text.
- **Safety:** the guarded web fetcher, the support panel and the redaction of other people's
  contact details. All protect from real harm and are now frozen.
- **The world's base:** one scene feeds both the moving and the still world, so the still
  version and accessibility come free once there's something to show.

Detail: appendix B, section 5.

### Where the effort went, and what it bought

- 46 fix rounds over 21 reviewed pieces of work; 151 of 294 commits are fixes (appendix C).
- **Best value:** wrong data shown to a person. Examples:
  - "ready now" for nursing without the nursing register's gate;
  - an unknown spending limit treated as passed;
  - "none found" for a page that was never read.

  These closed in one to three rounds.
- **Least value:**
  - about 10 rounds on language-rule edge cases;
  - three rounds on the wording of the slate's search notes.

  Each round found a new rare case. The loops ended only when a rule replaced the patching
  (ledger: "every note describes what the search did … never what ESCO contains").
- **The product owner's own complaints were under 1% of about 600 findings.** Those were plain
  words, too much helper text and one idea per screen. The words they called "psychobabble" are
  still on screen (`RoutePreview.tsx:119,123`: "Economic cost", "Direct spending").
- **"Small things wait for the final review" lapsed.** The rule was written for one stretch of
  the build, so the world work went back to fixing small things at once.

### Rules that cost a lot and protect little

- **The slate's search notes.** They are English sentences checked one by one for truth
  (`profile/exploration.py:910-1083`).
  - Cost: five fix rounds and about 3,750 lines.
  - What they protect: notes kept behind a disclosure, which no decision rests on.
  - Recommendation: the backend returns facts (found work, found none, not searched, and why).
    The web shows them from a small fixed set of plain sentences. Tests check the facts, not the
    words.
- **The slate's word-search rules** keep growing by exception: a one-word work style, "too
  general", main names only, hyphens (appendix B, F4). Recommendation:
  - freeze them;
  - the product owner judges suggestion quality on real examples;
  - later, let a person pick skills from a list, as titles already work.

### The world: where it serves the work and where it costs it

- **Today it shows two facts:** whether the person has answered, and their target titles
  (`lib/world/model.ts:42-55`).
- **What that costs:** about 3,000 lines of code, plus a hand-copied second drawing in the design
  references (`docs/design/compositions/world.js:58`).
- **On a desktop** it costs little and gives a sense of place.
- **On a phone** it works against the person:
  - it takes about half the first screen, and pushes the first decision out of view;
  - in one view its button covers the Suggestions tab, and the automated checks miss it
    (appendix E, F7 and F8).

  The plan's own rule says: "On a phone the world is the backdrop behind focused views, with
  the console and cards in sheets" (`development-plan.md:208`).
- **Recommendation:**
  - On a phone, the decision comes first.
  - Each chapter's working view (words, choices, numbers) is built first, and its world moment
    after.
  - Once a chapter exists, the app's own stories replace the hand-copied drawing of that place.

### What a person hits today (appendix E)

- **The demo interviewer loops.**
  - It understands six fixed patterns. Anything else gets "Thank you." and the same question
    again.
  - "I know my direction" repeats its question for ever, because there's no pattern for a
    target.
  - The demo is the only offline mode.
- **Choosing a target makes the career ideas look out of date.** "Suggest again" becomes the
  bright button, and nothing says what was saved.
- **Shared skills are counted, never named.** The "unexpected connection" is computed and never
  shown.
- **No screen shows the model budget or lets you raise it**, although the error tells you to.
- **Nobody has used the real-data live path through the app.** Every model call so far came
  from test tooling.

### Seams to fix before more chapters (appendix B)

- **A target is stored as plain text.** Four places turn it into an occupation, each with
  different rules. You, Skills and the world can disagree about the same target. Fix: work out
  each target's occupation once, and store it with its source and how far Careerwright can
  cover it.
- **The Options task hides a backend piece:** a store for about 15 money and time inputs. Split
  it out.
- **The route search's durable machinery may be more than L0 needs** (four roles, ten learning
  options). Size it after the core search is measured.

## 3. Roles beyond a standard list

### What the reviews found (appendix F)

- **Keeping roles to ESCO is our own rule.** The specification asks for "emerging roles absent
  from older taxonomies". It lets a model's title count once "an authoritative occupational/
  professional source or independent employer evidence" backs it (spec 261, 263). ADR 0007
  narrowed that to "only if ESCO holds it". The employer-evidence half was never built.
- **An official UK title list is already imported and licensed: the ONS coding index.** It
  holds 23,898 titles, 20,334 of them not in ESCO. Examples: "Registered nurse", "Data quality
  analyst", "Prompt engineer", "Retrofit coordinator", "Doula". Today it's used only for pay
  codes (`catalogue/soc.py`).
- **The model already suggests titles, and the app throws them away** as one line: "couldn't
  match … so not suggested" (`profile/exploration.py:818-825`). That line is the frustration
  the product owner described.
- **The best-covered roles are the ones the You chapter can't recognise.** Three of the four
  reviewed roles aren't ESCO titles, so their career ideas come up empty.
- **Downstream, a role outside ESCO loses almost nothing.** Every role outside the four
  reviewed ones is already "can't be assessed yet", ESCO roles included. Job search already
  takes any title.
- **Nothing in the web app uses the title search yet,** so changing this now costs no rework.

### The product owner's research agrees

We pin the same ESCO release it describes (v1.2.1). Its software-developer example holds in our
file: "perform scientific research" is listed as an essential skill.

It supports **ESCO as a shared vocabulary under a richer record of the real role**, which is
what the reviewed pack already does for its four roles. It conflicts with three parts of the
design, each fixed below:
- ESCO's "essential skills" alone drive several revelations;
- no one can say "none of these is my job";
- seniority and industry aren't kept.

### Recommendation: two steps now, deep research later

| Step | What the person gets | How it stays honest | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Your title, recognised** | The first question finds titles in ESCO and the ONS list. "Use my own words" is always accepted. Next: "Which of these is closest?" or "None of these is my job". The model's title ideas that ONS lists become suggestions, with the answers they came from. Optional seniority and industry beside a title | Exact match in a pinned, hash-checked file; nothing claimed beyond "ONS lists this title" | Free, offline, works in the demo |
| **2. Research this role** | For a title no list holds: one web search finds pages about the role. It counts once two independent sites name it (or one official site), with the quotes one step away | Code checks the title word for word on each page; only the title is sent, never the person's answers; records stay "not reviewed" | About 1–5 US cents a title on Spark, a few cents more on Wonder |
| Later (not L0) | One deep-research run that maps a person's whole role space | — | Breaks today's budget rules; multi-search runs are unqualified; slow and opaque. The specification puts it in M1 |

Fixes the research asks for, folded into step 1:
- Describe usual skills as "ESCO lists these for this kind of work".
- Let the person mark a skill "not part of my job".
- Never base a revelation on ESCO's essential skills alone.

**What later chapters can show for these roles:**

| Chapter | Shows |
|---|---|
| Skills | What the chosen closest occupation lists, plus what the person's chosen adverts ask for |
| Pay | The official ONS figures for the title's group, labelled as the group's |
| Roles | Real job listings |
| Options and routes | Only through decision 4 |

## 4. Is the plan still sensible?

### What the plan review found (appendix D)

- **The gate is backwards.** Session W2 requires every review score to reach at least 4
  before any other chapter is built. So the whole decision path waits for spectacle in the
  first chapter.
- **The world is already good enough to build on.** Design references exist for all six
  places, and they are plain panels over a simple world.
- **Most of the remaining world work is spectacle:** ignition, revelations lit, rings,
  previews, frame re-measuring.
- **Backend work waits behind interface work, though no session blocks it:** the route search
  core, and the planning inputs hidden in the Options task.
- **Human review was never the bottleneck.** Every session was held within hours. Build and
  review loops set the pace.
- **The sessions that worked were short and hands-on.** Long question sets were handed to an
  AI check, by the product owner's own ruling. Session W2's planned sheet has about 25 items,
  mostly matching settings.
- **The reason ADR 0008 gave for building Roles last no longer holds.** Live job data now
  works. Roles still comes after the routes, because routes don't need it.

### Three shapes compared

| | A. Current order: finish the world, then chapters | **B. Decision path first, moments after (recommended)** | C. Cut L0 to the decision path |
|---|---|---|---|
| First printable plan | after about 55+ agent hours | after about 50–80 (it now includes the role space) | a little sooner than B |
| Risk | Spectacle before substance; one low score loops before any chapter; route search risk found late | Plain chapters read as "bland" (mitigated: built to the references you called "OK") | Drops the opportunity map and replanning, which the specification requires |
| Keeps | Everything, in the old order | Everything; the order changes and the moments move later | Less |

**Why B:**
- It follows the product owner's direction and keeps their world.
- It moves the riskiest technical work forward.
- It puts the open role space where a person first meets it.
- C's cuts can still be offered at the end-to-end session, if time matters by then.

## 5. Muse Wonder for human testing (appendix G)

**Reading of "human testing":** the product owner using the live app with their own details,
in their own sessions. Automated checks keep the cheap test model.

**Facts:**
- **It's listed but has never been called.** `muse-wonder-1.0` costs $4.00 in, $0.20 cached and
  $20.00 out per million tokens (`identity/model_registry.py:83`). ADR 0003 left it unselected.
  The specification puts it in M1.
- **The switch already exists, and it's safe.** The production model is configuration
  (`CAREERWRIGHT_META_PRODUCTION_MODEL`). Wonder is a Standard (no-training) model, so personal
  data is allowed. The test model's synthetic-only safeguards don't change.
- **Today's limits would refuse it.** The default per-run limit, $0.15, was sized for Spark.
  On Wonder, three attempts at an interview turn can reach about $0.66, so every turn would be
  refused as over budget (`config.py:120-130`).
- **Calls the app makes during a person's use are not copied to the cost log.** Only the test
  commands write to it, and the cost rule says every call is logged.

**Estimates on Wonder, from measured Spark token counts:**

| Call | Cost |
|---|---|
| Interview turn | about 2 US cents |
| Career ideas | about 1½ US cents |
| Written "why" note | about 4 US cents |
| Web search | 3–6 US cents |
| A 2-hour session | about $1.50–2.00 (about £1.20–1.60) |

**What "yes" sets up (task 3):**
1. **Qualify it first.** Two calls on synthetic input, then at most six evaluation cases.
   Under $0.50 (about 40p).
2. **A session command** that starts the app on Wonder, with limits of about $5 a session
   (about £4) and $0.75 a run (about 60p).
3. **After each session, the cost log is updated.** Each call's model, kind, tokens and cost
   is copied in. Never its content.
4. **The records are updated.** ADR 0003, the specification's model section and the register
   say an M1 item was brought forward for these sessions only.
5. **Spark stays the release default.** Whether the release uses Wonder is a later decision,
   taken at the end-to-end session.

## 6. How the build is reviewed from here (appendix C)

- **Keep reviewing hard, and fix within the task:** anything that changes a decision (ready or
  not, feasible or not, unknown versus none, money, eligibility), lost input, fetch safety, the
  support panel, the first ten seconds.
- **Small things always wait** for the final review (this makes the expired rule permanent).
  The exceptions: a finding from the product owner's session, or a failing check.
- **One rule for every sentence a person reads:** it says what Careerwright did or checked,
  never what a source or list contains. When exact and plain conflict, say less.
- **The product owner judges suggestion quality** on a fixed set of real examples. Reviewers
  don't loop on it. At most one matching round per task.
- **Bound loops by kind of finding.** When one kind of finding comes back in two re-reviews in a
  row, the next step is a rule, a fail-safe or a one-line question to the product owner, never
  another patch. Wording, matching and visual work stop at three rounds; security, money and
  data keep five.
- **Ask the product owner before round 2 of any policy question.** Examples: empty versus
  noisy suggestions, strict versus relaxed rules.
- **Every review of a screen starts from the product owner's own session comments.** A comment
  can't be carried forward twice without asking them.
- **Severity is graded by its effect on the person.** Register, document and code-tidiness
  findings are batched, once per sprint and at the final review.
- **Smaller slices:** one chapter view or one backend contract each, keeping implementers well
  under their context limits.
- **Add the product owner's own commands to the acceptance run** (`make demo`, opening each
  session package). Fix the recurring worker-shutdown hang.
